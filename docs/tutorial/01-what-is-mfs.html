The people

MFS means mobile financial services. A customer opens a wallet tied to their mobile number (the MSISDN, for example 01711000001) and can then hold and move money with only a phone. Four kinds of wallet holders matter here:

WhoWhat they areHow they reach us
CustomerAn ordinary person with a wallet.The customer app, or a basic phone with USSD (dial the short code *268#, get text menus).
AgentA shop that swaps cash for wallet money. You give an agent paper money and it appears in your wallet (cash in), or the other way round (cash out).The agent app.
MerchantA shop or website that accepts wallet payments.The merchant app, or the web checkout on its website.
OrganisationA company that pays many people at once, for example salaries.The org portal: upload a file of payments.

Behind the scenes there are also operators: Bits staff who watch transactions and fix the rare stuck ones through the ops console.

The core: where the money lives

In plain English

The core is the bank vault and the accounting book. It knows every balance, writes every ledger entry, applies fees and limits and checks the PIN. It is a separate system owned by another team (Bits's "Digital Wallet" and "Digital Payment" services). The middleware never calculates or stores a balance. It only asks the core to do things and acts on the answer.

The middleware talks to the core by sending commands over Kafka (a message system, explained in chapter 3) and receiving events back. A command is a request such as "validate this transfer" or "move Tk 10 from A to B"; an event is the answer, such as "validated" or "rejected: wrong PIN". The full list is in chapter 3.

In this repository

The real core is not here. mw-core-mock plays it: it keeps its own small ledger, checks PINs and limits, charges fees and answers the same commands with the same events, so the middleware cannot tell the difference.

Partners

Some features need a company outside Bits:

  • Mobile network operators (MNOs: Grameenphone, Robi, Banglalink, Teletalk). They sell airtime (top-up) through their charging system, called the OCS, and they also run the USSD gateways that connect basic phones to us.
  • Bill aggregators: one company that collects bills for many utilities (electricity, gas, water).
  • Banks: for moving money between a bank account and a wallet.
  • An SMS centre (SMSC): delivers our text messages.

Each partner has its own API, its own error codes and its own ways of failing. The spec counts about 30 of them. In this repository all of them are faked by mw-simulators.

So what does the middleware do?

If the core holds the money and the partners do their own jobs, why is there a whole system in between? Because someone has to:

  1. Be the single front door. Apps, USSD, websites and portals all speak different protocols. The middleware receives them all, checks who is calling, and turns every request into one standard shape.
  2. Make sure nothing happens twice. Phones lose signal and retry. A retried "send Tk 10" must not send Tk 20. This is called idempotency.
  3. Run multi-step jobs in the right order. A top-up is: check the customer can pay, reserve the money, ask the operator for airtime, then either take the reserved money or give it back. If any step fails half-way, the money must end up right. This step-by-step job is called a saga.
  4. Never guess. When a partner or the core does not answer, the middleware does not know whether the money moved. It marks the payment UNKNOWN and keeps asking until it finds out (chapter 3).
  5. Speak every partner's language. One adapter per partner family translates our standard request into their API and their answer back.
  6. Tell people. SMS and email to the customer, signed callbacks to merchants.
  7. Keep a record. An append-only audit trail of everything, which no one can edit later.
  8. Give staff a console to search transactions and fix stuck ones, where every risky action needs a second person to approve it (maker-checker).

How money is written down

Money is never a decimal number in this code. It is an object with a whole number of the smallest unit and a currency:

{ "valueMinor": 1000, "currency": "BDT" }   // Tk 10.00

The Bangladeshi taka (BDT) has 100 poisha, so valueMinor is in poisha: Tk 10.00 is 1000, and the Tk 5 P2P fee charged by the core mock is 500. Whole numbers avoid rounding errors that decimals bring.
